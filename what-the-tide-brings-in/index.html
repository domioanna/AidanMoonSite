The Story

One island. One iron in the deep ground. One Leask left to answer for it.

Dan Leask comes home on the weekly ferry to bury his father from a closed casket, settle the debts, and be gone in two days. But the island that was bankrupt a year ago is freshly painted. The cannery runs a night shift. The harbour has gone a slick, iridescent black, and the nets come up full.

Dan spent eleven years on the floor of the North Sea. Now he can't stand on a pier and watch the tide come in, and he won't say why. Skerravoe doesn't care why. It needs him back in the water, because something is lying in the deep ground off the headland, and only one man alive has the training to reach it and the blood to lay a hand on it.

The Fisherman meets Starve Acre on a Scottish island — extraction horror where the monster keeps a ledger. For readers of John Langan, Andrew Michael Hurley and Julia Armfield's Our Wives Under the Sea.

The Island

Skerravoe. Everyone knows. Nobody says.

A small, jagged island off the far north of Scotland. One settlement, also called Skerravoe: salt-bleached houses, a shop that is also the post office, a kirk nobody fills, a harbour, and the Brae cannery rusting on the north pier. The ferry comes once a week, weather permitting. Off the headland the seabed drops into the deep ground, and the water in the harbour has gone a colour it shouldn't be.

haar
The sea fog that comes in off the water and stays. The minister doesn't cross in it. Neither does much else.
the Hjalti
The ferry. Flat-bottomed, room for three cars and a cattle pen. An hour and forty from the mainland, Saturdays, weather permitting.
the deep ground
The trench off the headland. The old name for it is the Sloch. The boats fish its edge and no further.
the tick book
The shop's credit ledger. Two years of close-written names and sums, and every line struck through in the one season.
the kirk
Empty. Long grass at the door. The font inside is dry, and on this island nobody touches fresh water if they can help it.
the rent
What the island calls its arrangement. You'd have to ask them about it. They won't tell you.

The People

The island doesn't do portraits.

You'll know them by what they keep, what they owe, and what they will not say.

The narrator

Daniel Leask

Eleven years a saturation diver on the floor of the North Sea. Now he can't stand on a pier and watch the tide come in, and he won't say why.

He came home for his father's funeral. The town says the sea took Eilert Leask. The town says a lot of things.

The shop & the post

Etta Flett

Runs the shop and the post office, which on an island this size means she runs the truth. Dan's oldest friend; her boy, Joss, learned his knots from Dan's father.

She knows what every house owes. She knows what every house paid with. She has stopped writing either down.

The old crew

Sandy Gunn

Seventy-odd, and dying of something ordinary. On this island, that makes him the lucky one.

He crewed Eilert Leask's boat for forty years, and he is the only man on Skerravoe who still eats the mainland food. Ask him why. He'll tell you. That's the problem.

The father

Eilert Leask

Buried without a minister, in a casket the joiner asked his son not to open. The island says he drowned. The island hasn't said much else.

He kept a logbook for forty years. His son is about to read it.

Old Bargains

Every coast in Britain had an arrangement like this once.

Skerravoe is invented. What it does is not. The novel grew out of real sea-lore from the north and west of these islands — the deals people made with the water when the land stopped feeding them. Four of them, with their sources.

Isle of Lewis · Hallowtide

Shony

In 1703 a traveller wrote down what the men of Ness did every Hallowtide. They gathered at the church with ale, and one of them waded into the sea with a cup of it and said:

"I give you this Cup of Ale, hoping that you'll be so kind as to send us plenty of sea-ware, for inriching our Ground the ensuing year."

He poured it into the waves. The sea's name was Shony. The kirk put a stop to it. Nobody asked the sea whether the account was closed.

Martin Martin, A Description of the Western Islands of Scotland (1703)

Peterhead · 19th century

The sea must have its number

Fishermen on the north-east coast of Scotland had a rule. If a man went over the side, you did not pull him out. Not out of cruelty. Out of arithmetic.

"The sea maun hae its nummer."

Save the drowning man, and it takes the one who saved him. The narrator of this novel was a commercial diver for eleven years. He knows exactly what the sea is owed.

A Peterhead saying, recorded in the nineteenth century

Newlyn & Mousehole · Cornwall

The Bucca's share

The fishermen of west Cornwall paid a landlord. At night they left part of their catch on the sand for the Bucca, the spirit of the sea and the storm. Pay, and the boats came home.

Every coast had an arrangement like this once. Most of them lapsed.

William Bottrell, Traditions and Hearthside Stories of West Cornwall (1870–80)

Orkney · the turn of the year

Sea Mither & Teran

In Orkney the sea has two masters. All summer it belongs to the Sea Mither, who calms the water and lets the fish breed. In winter it belongs to Teran, whose name means furious anger. Twice a year they fight for it.

In spring she wins. In autumn she is too tired, and Teran breaks free, and the storms come.

W. T. Dennison, The Scottish Antiquary (1890–91); Ernest Marwick, The Folklore of Orkney and Shetland

Skerravoe's arrangement didn't lapse.

The companion

The Deep Is One Deep

The sea gave the boat back. It kept the man.

Mae lives alone at Sterrin, four houses and a dead lighthouse on a bay at the top of Scotland. When her husband drowns off a flat calm, she can't bring herself to cut his creels loose. Four months later she rows out to do it at last — and every pot is packed full.
